Most owners choose a property manager the slow way: a few referrals, a round of calls, and three proposals that each describe the job differently. One quotes a percentage, another a flat fee, a third leaves out leasing altogether. Comparing them is guesswork.

A request for proposals, or RFP, fixes that by turning the process around. You describe the property and the job once, in writing, and every manager answers the same questions. This post covers what to put in a property management RFP and how to read what comes back.

## What an RFP is

An RFP is a document that says what you own, what you need done, and how you want companies to respond. It is common in commercial real estate and works just as well for an apartment building or a group of rental homes.

Its value is in the structure. When every bidder prices the same scope, the differences between them become visible.

## What to include

A manager can only price what you describe. The more specific the RFP, the fewer assumptions end up hidden in the bids.

- **The property.** Type, location, number of units or square footage, age and general condition.
- **Current status.** Occupancy, who manages it today, and any known problems such as deferred repairs or overdue rent.
- **Scope of services.** Leasing, rent collection, maintenance, accounting, inspections, and anything else you expect. Say what is in and what is out.
- **Reporting.** Which reports you want, how often, and whether you need access to the books.
- **Spending authority.** How much the manager may spend on a repair without asking you first.
- **Term and exit.** The contract length you have in mind and the notice you expect for ending it.
- **Timeline.** The deadline for proposals, when you will decide, and when you want management to start.

## Ask for answers in the same format

Tell bidders how to respond. Ask each one to state:

1. The management fee and what it is calculated on.
2. Every other charge: leasing and renewal fees, maintenance markups, setup or onboarding costs, and termination fees.
3. Service commitments, such as how quickly they answer tenants and owners and how they handle emergencies.
4. Experience with properties like yours, and who will be your day-to-day contact.
5. Licenses and insurance.

If one proposal leaves a line blank, that is a question to ask before you compare totals.

## How to compare the bids

The lowest management fee is not always the lowest cost. A small percentage with high leasing fees and repair markups can cost more over a year than a higher percentage that covers everything. Add up the likely annual cost of each bid under the same assumptions.

Then look past price. Response commitments, experience with your property type and the quality of the proposal itself all say something about how the company will treat your building. A vague proposal often leads to a vague relationship.

Before you sign, speak to references, read the full management agreement, and have an attorney review it. Licensing rules for property managers vary by state, so confirm that the company holds what your state requires.
