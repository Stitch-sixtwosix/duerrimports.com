When you buy imported products, the number that matters is not the price of the goods. It is what each unit costs once it is sitting in your stockroom, ready to use or sell. That figure is called the landed cost.

Two quotes can show the same product price and still lead to very different landed costs. This post explains what goes into the term, where buyers tend to get surprised, and how to compare offers fairly.

## What landed cost means

Landed cost is the total cost of getting a product from the supplier abroad to your door, divided across the units you receive. It includes the product itself and everything it took to move it, clear it and deliver it.

For a café buying coffee or an office buying supplies, it is the honest answer to a simple question: what does one bag, box or case really cost us?

## What usually goes into it

The exact list depends on the product and the route, but most landed costs are built from the same parts:

- **The product.** The price the supplier charges for the goods.
- **Packing and preparation.** Cartons, pallets, labels and any work needed before the goods ship.
- **International freight.** Moving the goods from the origin country to the United States, by sea or air.
- **Insurance.** Cover for loss or damage while the goods are in transit.
- **Duties and taxes.** Charges set by the government on imported goods. The rate depends on the product's classification and origin, and rules change, so check the current position with a customs broker or trade advisor.
- **Customs clearance.** Broker fees and any inspection or port charges needed to release the shipment.
- **Domestic delivery.** Trucking from the port or airport to your address, including any extra charge for a liftgate or a set delivery window.

Add those together, divide by the number of usable units, and you have your landed cost per unit.

## Where buyers get surprised

Most surprises come from costs that were real all along but were left out of the first number someone looked at.

### The quote covered only part of the trip

A supplier abroad may quote a price that ends at their factory, at their port, or on board a ship. Everything after that point is still your cost, even if it appears on a different invoice. Ask where a quote stops.

### Small orders carry heavy fixed costs

Some costs, like clearance fees, stay roughly the same whether a shipment is large or small. Spread across a few cases, they add a lot to each unit. Spread across many, they add little. This is one reason importers ask about quantity before they quote.

### Not every unit arrives usable

If some goods arrive damaged or expire before you use them, the units you can actually sell carry the cost of the ones you could not. Storage space and shelf life belong in the calculation too.

## How to compare two offers

When you have more than one option, put them on the same footing before deciding:

1. Ask each seller exactly what the price includes, from pickup to delivery.
2. List the costs that are not included, and estimate them.
3. Use the same order quantity for every option.
4. Divide each total by the number of units you expect to use.
5. Compare delivery times as well, since a later arrival can cost you sales.

The lowest product price does not always give the lowest landed cost. A clear, complete quote is often worth more than a low partial one.

## A note on duties and tax

Import duties, classification and sales tax depend on the product, its origin and your business. This post is general information, not legal or tax advice. Talk to a customs broker or accountant about the rules that apply to you.
