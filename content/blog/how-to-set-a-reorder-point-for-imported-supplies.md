When a product comes from a local distributor, running low is an inconvenience. You call, and it arrives in a day or two. When a product is imported, running low can mean weeks without it, because the next shipment has to be made, packed, shipped and cleared before it reaches you.

A reorder point solves this. It is the stock level at which you place your next order, set high enough that the new shipment lands before the old one runs out. This post explains how to work one out without special software.

## The three numbers you need

A reorder point comes from three figures:

1. **How fast you use the product.** Count how many units you go through in a normal week. A café might count bags of beans or cases of cups. An office might count boxes of supplies. A reseller counts units sold.
2. **How long a new order takes to arrive.** This is the lead time: from the day you place the order to the day the goods are on your shelf, not the day they ship.
3. **How much extra you want as a cushion.** This is safety stock, the amount you keep in case something is late or demand jumps.

The basic formula is:

> Reorder point = weekly use x lead time in weeks + safety stock

If you use 20 units a week, a new order takes 6 weeks to arrive, and you want 2 weeks of cushion, your reorder point is 20 x 6 + 40, or 160 units. When stock falls to 160, you order. These figures are only an example; use your own.

## Getting the lead time right

Lead time is where most estimates go wrong, because it has several parts:

- Time to confirm the order and any payment.
- Production time, if the goods are made or packed to order.
- Transit time by sea, air or ground.
- Time to clear customs and any inspections.
- Final delivery from the port or warehouse to you.

White-label orders often take longer than wholesale ones, because labels and packaging have to be printed and approved before production. If you are switching from one to the other, recheck the lead time.

Ask your supplier for a realistic range rather than a single best case, and plan around the longer end.

## Choosing your safety stock

Safety stock is a judgment call. More of it protects you from delays but ties up cash and shelf space. Less of it frees both but leaves you exposed.

A few questions help:

- How bad is a stockout? Running out of a café's main coffee is worse than running out of a seasonal item.
- How steady is your use? If sales swing a lot from week to week, keep more.
- How reliable has the route been? If past shipments have arrived late, keep more.
- Are there known slow periods? Holidays at the origin or at the port can add time.

## Check it against your storage and cash

A reorder point tells you when to order. Order quantity is a separate decision. Larger orders can lower the cost per unit, but every unit has to be stored and paid for before it is sold or used.

Before you settle on a number, check that you have room for the full order plus your safety stock, and that the product will keep for as long as it sits on the shelf. Coffee, food and anything with a best-by date need extra care here.

## Review it every few months

Use changes. A new location, a new menu item or a busy season can make an old reorder point too low. Set a reminder to recount your weekly use and recheck lead times every few months, and after any shipment that arrived noticeably late.

A simple routine works for most small buyers:

1. Count stock on the same day each week.
2. Compare it with the reorder point.
3. Order when you reach it, not when you are nearly out.
4. Note the date each order arrives, so your lead time stays accurate.
