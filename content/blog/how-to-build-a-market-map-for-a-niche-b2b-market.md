If you sell to a narrow market, such as a type of manufacturer, a kind of clinic or a specialist contractor, broad advertising often does not work well. The people you need are few, they are busy, and most of the audience an ad reaches will never buy from you.

The alternative is to start from the other end. Instead of reaching as many people as possible and hoping the right ones respond, you list every company that could realistically buy from you, then decide which of them to approach and when. That list, with the right details attached, is a market map.

This guide explains what a market map is, how to build a first version, and how to keep it from going stale.

## What a market map is

A market map is a structured list of the companies in your market, with enough information on each one to judge fit and plan an approach. It is not a contact list bought in bulk. It is closer to an inventory of your whole market.

A useful map answers three questions for every company on it:

- Does this company fit what we sell?
- How large an opportunity is it?
- Who inside the company would be involved in a purchase?

## Step 1: Write down who your best customers are

Before you collect any data, describe the companies that have bought from you and stayed happy. Look for what they share. Common traits include industry, size, location, the equipment or software they use, how they are regulated, and the problem that made them look for a supplier.

Keep the description specific. "Mid-size manufacturers" is too broad. "Food processors with one to three plants in the Southeast that run their own packaging lines" gives you something you can actually search for.

## Step 2: Choose your sources

Most of the information you need about businesses is public, though scattered. Useful places to look include:

- State business registries
- Industry associations and their member directories
- Trade show exhibitor lists
- Licensing and permit records, where your market is regulated
- Public procurement records, if you sell to organizations that buy through tenders
- Company websites and job postings
- Professional networking profiles

Pick the two or three sources that cover your market best, and start there. You can add more later.

## Step 3: Decide what to record

Agree on a short set of fields before you start, so every entry is comparable. A first version might include company name, location, size, the trait that makes it a fit, a fit score, and the names and roles of the people likely to be involved in a purchase.

Resist the urge to record everything. A field nobody uses is a field nobody keeps up to date.

## Step 4: Score and sort

Not every company on the map deserves the same effort. A simple scoring system helps. For example, give each company a score for fit and a score for size, and sort by the total.

The result is usually a small group of strong prospects, a larger middle group, and a tail of companies that fit only loosely. That tells you where to spend time first.

## Step 5: Map the people, not just the companies

B2B purchases are rarely made by one person. Someone uses the product, someone approves the budget, and someone signs. For your top accounts, note who fills each role, so your outreach does not depend on reaching a single contact.

## Step 6: Keep it current

A market map starts aging the day you finish it. People change jobs, companies open and close sites, and new competitors appear. Set a regular schedule to review the top of the list, and record when each entry was last checked.

This is also where a map becomes more than a list. When you track changes at the companies on it, such as new hires, new permits or a new executive, you can see which accounts are moving and time your outreach to match.

## A note on outreach rules

Contacting businesses by email, phone or mail is regulated, and the rules differ by country and state. Check what applies to you before you start, and consult a professional if you are unsure.
