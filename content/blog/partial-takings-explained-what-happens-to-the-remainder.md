Many eminent domain cases do not involve a whole property. A road widening takes a strip along the front of a lot. A new interchange takes a corner. A utility line needs an easement across the back. The owner keeps most of the land, but what is left may not be worth what it was.

This is a partial taking, and the land the owner keeps is usually called the remainder. For an attorney, the remainder is often where the most important questions in the case sit. This post walks through them in plain terms.

## What a partial taking is

In a partial taking, the government acquires only part of a parcel, or only some rights in it. That can mean:

- A strip of land in fee, such as extra width for a road
- A permanent easement, such as for drainage or utilities
- A temporary construction easement that ends when the work is done
- A change in access, such as a closed driveway or a new median

Each of these can appear on a right-of-way plan, and each raises different questions about value.

## The part taken and the damage to the rest

Just compensation in a partial taking generally has two pieces. The first is the value of the land or rights actually taken. The second is any loss in value to the remainder caused by the taking. That second piece is often called severance damages.

How these are measured depends on the jurisdiction. Some states compare the value of the whole property before the taking with the value of the remainder after it. Others value the part taken separately and then add damages to the remainder. The rules differ in detail, so the method that applies to a given case is a question for an attorney who practices in that state.

## Common ways a remainder loses value

Severance damages come from the way the taking changes the property that is left. Some common examples:

- **Access.** A closed curb cut or a new median can make a commercial site harder to reach.
- **Shape and size.** A lot that becomes narrow or irregular may no longer fit its current use or what zoning allows.
- **Parking and setbacks.** A taken strip can push a building closer to the new right of way, or remove required parking spaces.
- **Improvements.** Signs, wells, septic systems, fences and landscaping in the taken area may need to be moved or replaced.
- **Proximity.** A larger road, more noise or a change in grade can affect how the remainder can be used.

Whether a given effect is compensable, and how much it is worth, is a legal and appraisal question. Some states also allow benefits from the project to offset damages to the remainder.

## Why the plans matter

Most of these questions can be seen early, on the project's right-of-way plans, long before an offer letter arrives. The plans show which part of a parcel is needed, whether an easement is permanent or temporary, and where access changes. An attorney who reviews them early can identify the issues an owner may not notice on a first reading of an offer.

Owners in a partial taking often focus on the price for the strip being taken. The larger question can be what happens to everything else.

## A checklist for reviewing a partial taking

1. Identify exactly what is being taken: land in fee, a permanent easement, a temporary easement, or access rights.
2. Compare the remainder's size, shape and frontage before and after.
3. Check zoning, setbacks and parking requirements against the new boundaries.
4. List improvements in or near the taken area.
5. Note changes in access, grade or traffic flow.
6. Ask whether the offer addresses damages to the remainder at all.

This is general information, not legal advice. Owners and attorneys should rely on counsel and appraisers familiar with the rules in their state.
