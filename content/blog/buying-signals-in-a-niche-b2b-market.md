If you sell to a small, specialist market, you probably already know most of the companies that could buy from you. The hard part is not finding them. It is knowing which of them is ready to buy this quarter, and who inside the company makes the call.

That is what a buying signal is for. This post explains the term, lists the signals worth watching, and gives a short checklist for acting on one.

## What a buying signal is

A buying signal is a public event at a company that tends to come before a purchase. It does not prove the company will buy. It tells you that something has changed, and that a relevant message now has a reason to be read.

The difference matters in a niche market. A generic message sent to every company on your list is easy to ignore. A message that refers to something the company did last week is much harder to dismiss, because it is about them.

## Six signals worth tracking

[LeadGenAI](https://leadgenai.io/), which builds lead systems for niche B2B markets, lists these on its site as the events it watches:

- **Hiring.** A job posting shows where a company is putting money. A new role in operations, compliance or engineering often means a project is starting.
- **Permits.** A filed permit is an early, public sign of new construction, new equipment or an expansion.
- **Funding.** New money usually comes with a plan to spend it.
- **New leaders.** A new executive often reviews existing suppliers and tools in the first months.
- **Vendor switches.** A company that has just changed one supplier may be open to changing others.
- **RFPs.** A request for proposals is the most direct signal there is, though by then your competitors can see it too.

Not every signal matters in every market. A permit means a lot to an industrial supplier and little to a software company. The useful work is deciding which two or three events tend to come before a sale in your own niche.

## Where the signals come from

Most of this information is public. LeadGenAI says its data comes from public sources and licensed databases, including company registries, job boards, permit and procurement records, funding announcements, websites and professional profiles.

The difficulty is volume, not access. Checking a few thousand companies across that many sources every week is more than a sales team can do by hand, which is why the tracking is usually automated.

## A signal is not a contact

Knowing that a company is moving is half the job. The other half is knowing who to talk to.

Larger B2B purchases are rarely one person's decision. LeadGenAI's site puts a typical buying group at six to ten people. Reaching one of them is a start, but a deal usually needs several of them to agree. So each signal should be paired with a map of the people involved: who uses the product, who approves the budget, and who signs.

## A checklist for acting on a signal

1. Confirm the company fits your customer profile. A signal at the wrong company is noise.
2. Identify the trigger in one plain sentence, such as "they posted a plant manager role on Monday."
3. List the people in the buying group and what each one is likely to care about.
4. Write an opening that refers to the trigger and says why it is relevant to them. Keep it short.
5. Choose the channel that suits the person: email, LinkedIn, phone or mail.
6. Record what happened in your CRM, so you can see later which signals led to meetings.

Outreach by email, phone and mail is regulated, and the rules vary by country and state. Check the rules that apply to you, and consult a professional if you are unsure.
