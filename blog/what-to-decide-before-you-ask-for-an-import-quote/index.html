What to decide before you ask for an import quote

A short checklist for cafés, offices and resellers that want an accurate quote on imported products the first time they ask.

November 23, 2026 · Duerr Imports · 3 min read

When a café, office or shop asks an importer for a quote, the first reply is often a list of questions. That is normal. A price on imported goods depends on details the buyer usually knows but has not written down yet.

Answering those questions before you ask saves a round of emails and gets you a number you can actually plan around. This checklist covers what to decide first.

1. What you need, as specifically as you can say it

"Coffee" or "paper cups" is a starting point, not a specification. Write down what matters to you about the product:

  • the type or grade you want, and any you would rule out,
  • size, weight or pack format,
  • anything your customers or staff care about, such as origin or packaging material,
  • a product you already use and like, if you have one, so the importer can match it.

If you are not sure, say so. An importer can suggest options, but only if they know what the product is for.

2. Your brand or theirs

Decide whether you want the product as it comes, or with your own name on it. Buying existing products in quantity is wholesale. Having a product sourced and labeled with your brand is white label.

The two lead to different quotes. White label involves label design, artwork approval and usually a larger first order. If you are still choosing, our earlier post on wholesale or white label walks through the difference.

3. How much, and how often

Quantity drives price more than almost anything else, so give a realistic figure:

  • how many units you expect to need in a typical month,
  • whether this is a one-time order or a recurring one,
  • whether demand changes by season.

A rough range is fine. "Somewhere between two and four cases a month" is far more useful than no number at all.

4. When you need it

Imported goods take longer to arrive than goods from a domestic distributor, because they travel from abroad and pass through customs. If you have a launch date, a seasonal menu or an event, say so up front. The earlier the importer knows the date, the more options there are for meeting it.

5. Where it is going

Give the delivery address and anything that affects it: a loading dock or not, delivery hours, whether it should go to one location or several. Shipping costs and options depend on this.

6. What you need on paper

Some buyers need specific documents, such as ingredient lists, origin information or labeling that meets the rules for their kind of business. Rules for food, beverages and other regulated products vary, so check with your own advisor or local authority about what applies to you, and tell the importer what you need to receive.

7. How you plan to store it

Buying in quantity only helps if you have room for it. Think about shelf space, shelf life and storage conditions before you settle on an order size. A slightly higher unit price on a smaller order can cost less overall than stock that expires before you use it.

A short version you can send

If you want to keep it brief, an email with these lines is enough to start:

  1. business name and type (café, office, reseller),
  2. the product, with any must-haves,
  3. wholesale or white label,
  4. rough monthly quantity and whether it repeats,
  5. the date you need it by,
  6. delivery address.
