How to set nightly prices for a short-term rental

A practical way to build a nightly rate for a short-term rental, from your cost floor to seasons, weekdays and minimum stays.

November 24, 2026 · Duerr Imports · 3 min read

Most new hosts set one nightly rate, copy it from a listing down the street, and leave it alone. That is easy, but it usually means the calendar fills too fast in busy weeks and sits empty in slow ones.

A better price is built in layers. Start with what a night has to earn, check it against the market, then adjust it for the calendar. This guide walks through each step.

Start with your floor

Before you look at what anyone else charges, work out the lowest rate you can accept. Add up what the property costs you in a typical month:

  • Mortgage or rent, building fees and insurance
  • Utilities, internet and streaming services
  • Cleaning and laundry for each turnover
  • Supplies such as toiletries, coffee and paper goods
  • Platform fees and any management fee
  • Repairs and replacing worn items over time

Divide the fixed monthly costs by the number of nights you realistically expect to book, then add the per-stay costs spread across an average stay. The result is your floor. A rate below it loses money even when the unit is full.

Taxes on rental income and any lodging taxes depend on where the property is and how you rent it. Ask an accountant or tax professional what applies to you and build it into the floor.

Check the market

Next, look at listings that a guest would actually compare with yours. Match on location, number of bedrooms, number of guests, and the features that matter in your area, such as parking, a workspace or a pool.

Look at the rates those listings show for specific dates, not just their headline price. A listing that looks cheap on a Tuesday may cost much more on a Saturday. Note which comparable units are already booked for upcoming dates; that tells you more about demand than the prices alone.

Your base rate should sit somewhere in that range, above your floor. If the market range is below your floor, that is a signal to revisit costs or to consider longer stays instead.

Adjust for the calendar

A single base rate is only the starting point. Demand moves, and so should the price.

  1. Seasons. Set higher rates for your area's busy months and lower ones for quiet months.
  2. Days of the week. Leisure markets often fill on weekends; business travel markets often fill midweek. Price the strong days higher.
  3. Events and holidays. Local festivals, conferences and long weekends can justify a clear premium. Mark them on your calendar months ahead.
  4. Lead time. If a date is close and still open, a modest discount can fill it. If dates far out are booking quickly, your rate is probably too low.

Use minimum stays and fees with care

Minimum stays protect you from short bookings that cost more in cleaning than they earn. A two or three night minimum on weekends is a common approach, with shorter minimums in quiet periods to fill gaps.

Cleaning fees cover turnover costs, but guests see the total price. A high fee on a one night stay can make your listing look expensive next to others. Many hosts fold part of the cost into the nightly rate instead.

Weekly and monthly discounts can bring in longer stays, which mean fewer turnovers and steadier income. Check local rules first, since the rules for stays of a month or more can differ from those for nightly rentals.

Review prices on a schedule

Pricing is not a one time task. Once a week, look at the next few months: which dates are booked, which are open, and how your rates compare with similar listings. Raise prices where you are filling too early and lower them where open nights are coming up soon.

Keep a simple record of what you charged and what booked. After one full year you will have your own seasonal pattern to work from, which is more useful than any general rule.
